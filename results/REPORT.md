alpha = 0.4597 (95% CI [0.4452, 0.4729]). That is below Krippendorff's 0.667 floor for tentative conclusions and well below the 0.800 floor for firm ones. Three annotators agreed exactly with each other on 64.4% of pairwise comparisons. The interval is narrow, so this is a precisely measured moderate reliability, not an uncertain one.

**Kappa paradox test.** The kappa paradox does NOT fire. Raw agreement is 0.6439, far below the 0.85 the paradox requires, the AC1-kappa gap is 0.0092 rather than the >0.25 it requires, and the most common category holds only 40.5% of the mass, so no category is dominant enough to collapse kappa's chance model. All three chance-corrected coefficients agree with each other to within 0.0092. This rules out prevalence skew as the explanation for the moderate alpha and leaves genuine disagreement about the construct. The practical consequence is that the usual remedies for a low coefficient -- stratified oversampling of a rare class, or annotator retraining -- would not move this number.

### Accuracy ceiling and minimum detectable effect

The ceiling on the 3-way task is 81.4%: even a perfect model of what this annotator pool believes would disagree with a randomly chosen annotator on 18.6% of posts. On the binary task the ceiling is 89.4%. Reported 3-way accuracies in the high 60s therefore sit closer to the achievable ceiling than to 100%, and the headroom a leaderboard implies is largely not there.

On a test split of 2,015 posts, with two systems disagreeing on 20% of items, the smallest detectable accuracy difference at 80% power is 0.0279 on the observed scale -- 2.8 accuracy points -- and 0.0329 (3.3 points) once the difference is expressed in true-score units by dividing by sqrt(gold reliability). A published gap of 2% is therefore inside the noise floor of that design: detecting it reliably needs n >= 3,925 items, against the 2,015 available. Evaluated on the full 20,148-post corpus the same comparison resolves 0.0088 (0.9 points), so the gap is detectable there -- the constraint is the size of the split, not the corpus.

*This MDE covers item-sampling variance, and reports the attenuation correction separately. It does NOT cover gold-panel resampling variance: the labels are one realisation of a three-annotator draw, and a different draw from the same pool would produce different gold labels for a substantial share of posts. That variance is not identifiable from a single panel of three, so it is not estimated here rather than being guessed at. The observable proxy is in the consensus structure -- 51.1% of posts sit at a margin of one vote -- and it means the figures below are a floor on the real detection threshold, not a ceiling.*

## Study B -- judge validation with a held-out human

> The judge in this study is a held-out human annotator, not a language model. No model was called and this package makes no network requests. The purpose is to exercise the scoring harness end to end on real labels and to establish the ceiling an LLM judge would be measured against. Nothing here is an evaluation of any model's performance.

A real human annotator, scored against two of their peers, matches the panel on 76.1% of the 12,939 posts where those two peers agreed, with a judge-panel alpha of 0.6255. The two-annotator panel itself reaches consensus on only 64.2% of posts, so more than a third of the corpus has no target label under this design at all. With no conditioning on panel agreement the same human matches an individual panel member 64.5% of the time. Correcting the judge-panel alpha for the panel's own unreliability lifts it to 0.7882. Those are the numbers an LLM judge on this task should be measured against -- not 1.0, and not 76.1% read as though the missing 23.9% were the judge's fault.

**Ceiling.** A judge on this task should be measured against 76.1%, not against 100%. That is what a real human annotator from the same pool scores against two of their peers on the posts where those peers agreed. Against a single randomly drawn annotator across all posts, the best any predictor can do is 81.4%. The missing 23.9% is disagreement among the humans, and no judge, prompt, or model scale removes it. The arithmetic upper bound against a two-rater consensus is exactly 1.0 and is meaningless, because the consensus is not a function of the text while a judge is.

**Disattenuation.** Observed judge-panel alpha is 0.6255. The panel it is scored against has a reliability of only 0.6298, so the observed number is attenuated: it is measuring the judge through a noisy instrument. Correcting for the panel alone -- the right correction when the judge is a deterministic model -- gives 0.7882. That gap, 0.6255 to 0.7882, is the size of the mistake made by quoting the uncorrected number: a judge sitting at 0.63 looks mediocre and is in fact tracking the construct at roughly 0.79, and teams keep tuning judges that already hit the ceiling because of it.

**Harness validation.** The held-out human is just an annotator, so scoring them against each panel member with no conditioning must reproduce the corpus-wide pairwise agreement to within sampling noise. It uses two of the three pairs per post rather than all three, so exact equality is not expected. If these two numbers diverged, the panel construction would be leaking the judge's own label or dropping posts non-randomly -- the two failure modes that make a judge harness report a flattering number. They agree, so the scoring path is sound end to end, which is the entire purpose of running a stand-in judge before spending inference budget on a real one. Corpus pairwise agreement 0.6439, harness figure 0.6447.

## Study C -- replication economics

Single-rater reliability is 0.4597; the 3-rater aggregate the corpus actually publishes is 0.7185. Getting to 0.667 takes 3 raters, getting to 0.800 takes 5. But on a fixed label budget, aggregate statistical power is maximised at ONE rater per item -- at any reliability, as an analytic property of the attenuation model -- so a third rater is not worth more than a third more items if the only goal is detecting a difference between two systems. Replication earns its cost through measurability and per-item defensibility, not through power, and a budget argument that conflates the two will reach a confident wrong answer in whichever direction it started.

At this dataset's variance and a 60,444-label budget, spending on one rater per item gives 60,444 items and a true-scale MDE of 0.0075. Spending on three raters gives 20,148 items and 0.0104 -- 39% worse. The three-rater gold label is more reliable (0.7185 against 0.4597), but not by enough to pay for having a third as many items. For the narrow question 'which design detects a smaller true difference between two systems', more items wins, and it wins at every reliability.

**Why replicate anyway.**

- With k=1 the reliability of the labels is unknowable. There is no second judgement to compare against, so no alpha, no MDE that accounts for attenuation, and no way to detect that the pool has drifted. The power calculation that recommends k=1 depends on a rho_1 that only replication can supply.
- Per-item gold quality does not follow aggregate power. Error analysis, safety triage, per-community breakdowns and any use of individual rows all need a label that is defensible for that row, and a k=1 label is one person's opinion with no margin at all.
- A single-rated corpus cannot distinguish a hard item from a careless annotator, so it has no quality-control signal and no way to identify the genuinely ambiguous items that are usually the most informative ones.
- Aggregate power is not the only objective. If the deliverable is a public benchmark that others will rank models on, the per-item defensibility of the labels is the product.

At an assumed 0.08 USD per label, the published 3-rater design costs 4,835.52 USD for a gold reliability of 0.7185. Reaching the 0.800 firm-conclusion floor needs 5 raters, 8,059.20 USD -- an extra 3,223.68 USD for +0.0912 of reliability. Whether that is worth paying depends on what the labels are for, which is exactly the conversation the number is meant to start.
